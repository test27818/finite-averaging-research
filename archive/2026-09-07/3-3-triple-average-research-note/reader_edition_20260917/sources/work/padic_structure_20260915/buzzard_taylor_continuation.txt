         Analytic continuation of overconvergent
                       eigenforms.
                                     Kevin Buzzard∗

                                         Abstract
         Let f be an overconvergent p-adic eigenform of level N pr , r ≥ 1, with
      non-zero Up -eigenvalue. We show how f may be analytically continued
      to a subset of X1 (N pr )an containing, for example, all the supersingular
      locus. Using these results we extend the main theorem of [BT] to many
      ramified cases.


Introduction
Firstly we give an overview of our main results and applications. Let r ≥ 1 be an
integer, let p be a prime, and let N ≥ 5 be a positive integer prime to p. Let k be
an integer and let f be an overconvergent p-adic modular form of weight k and
level N pr . That is, f is a section of ω ⊗k over a rigid analytic subspace of X1 (N pr )
strictly containing the connected component of the ordinary locus containing the
cusp ∞ (we shall be more precise about these notions in Section 1 below). We
show that if Up f = ap f for some non-zero p-adic number ap then f can be
analytically continued to a section of ω ⊗k over a rather large analytic subspace
of X1 (N pr ). This subspace can be succinctly described as the complement of
the component of the ordinary locus which contains the cusp 0. Note that this
subspace contains the entire supersingular locus, and if r > 1 then it contains a
lot of the ordinary locus as well.

1     Modular curves.
In this section we define arithmetic models of the modular curves that we shall
be interested in. This section is just a collection of results that we shall need
later; everything here has been known to the experts for about 15 years, and a
reference for this entire section is [KM].
    Let p be a prime, and let N ≥ 5 be an integer prime to p. In this sec-
tion we recall the definitions and basic properties of certain modular curves.
Firstly we remark that there are two conventions currently in use for defining
the curve X1 (M ), where M is a positive integer. One possibility is to think
of the non-cuspidal locus of X1 (M ) as parameterising elliptic curves equipped
with a point of order M, and the other is to think of it as parameterising elliptic
curves equipped with embeddings of µM . It turns out that we shall be doing
computations involving the Tate curve, and that it is convenient to use embed-
dings of µM when M is prime to p. However, because we will be considering
certain models of these curves over bases in which p is not invertible, it is more
convenient to stick with the notation of [KM] and use points of order M when

rather non-standard, but only differ by twists from either of the usual defini-
tions. In particular, if our base contains an M th root of unity then all the curves
we use are isomorphic to the modular curves defined by either of the standard
conventions.

modular forms are equal if we know that they have equal q-expansions.

3     The theory of the canonical subgroup.
In this section we review and extend the theory of the canonical subgroup. Many
of the results here appear in [K] (for the canonical subgroup of order p) and [G]
(for the extension to canonical subgroups of order pn ). These latter references
put mild assumptions on p, which we remove.
    As before, let p be a prime and let N ≥ 5 be an integer prime to p. Let
W denote the ring of Witt vectors of Fp , and let K0 be its field of fractions.
Let K be any finite field extension of K0 , and let OK denote the integers of
K. We shall identify the residue fields of K and W with Fp . Normalise the
norm on K so that |p| = p−1 . We know that K is complete with respect to this
norm, and hence there is a theory of rigid analytic spaces over K. We shall use
the foundations of rigid analysis as set up in [BGR]. The only notation that
we shall use here that is not defined in [BGR] is that of a wide-open subspace
of a smooth curve, which is defined in [Col2] in the generality that we need.
For the purposes of this paper, a wide-open space is a rigid-analytic space over
K that is isomorphic to an admissible open in a smooth proper curve over K
whose complement is, after a finite extension of K if necessary, isomorphic to a
disjoint union of a non-zero finite number of affinoid discs.
    The non-cuspidal points on X1 (N )K parameterise elliptic curves (with extra
structure), and in this section we shall explain something about the region of
X1 (N )K corresponding to the elliptic curves with supersingular reduction. This
region is neither Zariski-open nor closed, but it is an admissible open subset of
the rigid analytic space X1 (N )an over K associated to X1 (N )K , and we shall
use rigid analysis to understand this region more clearly.

    There are a finite non-zero number of supersingular points in the special fibre
of X1 (N )OK , that is, points which correspond to supersingular elliptic curves
equipped with a point of order N. Let x ∈ X1 (N )OK (Fp ) = X1 (N )W (Fp ) be a
supersingular point. Then the completion of the local ring OX1 (N )W ,x is a W -
algebra which is non-canonically isomorphic to W [[Tx ]] for some parameter Tx .
Fix such an isomorphism. The subset Bx of X1 (N )an consisting of points which
reduce to x is a rigid space, isomorphic to the open unit disc, and the choice of Tx
can be thought of as a choice of a parameter on this disc. Indeed, we can think
of Tx as an isomorphism of rigid spaces from Bx to the open unit disc in K. The
fact that Bx is indeed a disc seems to be known to the experts, although a lot
of the literature on this subject appears to only consider the case when the base
field K is algebraically closed (which is clearly not true here). One reference
which proves everything in the generality we need is Propositions 0.3.5 and 0.2.7
of [Be].
    If 0 < r ≤ 1 is of the form pq with q a rational number, then define Bx,<r
to be the subdisc of Bx consisting of points P ∈ Bx such that |Tx (P )| < r. In
general this disc will depend on the choice of Tx . However, the only W -algebra
automorphisms of W [[Tx ]] send Tx to ap + uTx , where a ∈ W and u ∈ W [[Tx ]]
is a unit, and this implies that for r > 1/p the disc Bx,<r only depends on x
and r.

of ∪x∈SS Bx,<r in X1 (N )an , where SS denotes the (finite non-empty) set of
supersingular points. Then X1 (N )≥r is the intersection of finitely many affi-
noids in a separated space and is hence affinoid. We shall refer to X1 (N )≥1 =
X1 (N )an \ ∪x∈SS Bx as the ordinary locus of X1 (N )an and to the X1 (N )≥r for
r < 1 as a system of strict neighbourhoods of X1 (N )≥1 . If ω an denotes the ana-
lytification of the sheaf ω on X1 (N )K , and if k is an integer, then the Banach
space H 0 (X1 (N )≥1 , (ω an )⊗k ) is the space of p-adic modular forms of weight k,
and the union of the subspaces H 0 (X1 (N )≥r , (ω an )⊗k ) for r < 1 as above is the
space of overconvergent p-adic forms of weight k.
    If N ≤ 4 then there is still a theory, but one must be slightly more careful
because the sheaf ω may not exist. In this case, one may proceed by adding
an auxiliary level structure, working at higher level, and then taking invariants.
The structure of the supersingular discs might be slightly more complicated
if N ≤ 4, and we shall avoid the problem completely by working only at level
N ≥ 5, and using the trick above to deal with smaller levels when necessary.
    We now briefly explain how this approach to p-adic modular forms relates to
the one described by Katz in [K] and by Gouvêa in [G]. Katz and Gouvêa use
the Eisenstein series Ep−1 to give a parameter near each supersingular point.
Because Ep−1 is not a true modular form when p ≤ 3, Katz has to put some extra
conditions on N in these cases, and Gouvêa avoids small primes completely.
If Ep−1 exists then it is a section of ω ⊗(p−1) on X1 (N )W . If x is a supersingular
point then choose an open affine U ⊆ X1 (N )W over which ω ⊗(p−1) becomes
isomorphic to OU . Then Ep−1 can be thought of as a section of OU (U ) and
it is known that Ep−1 mod p is the Hasse invariant, which has a simple zero

Our approach of choosing Tx arbitrarily has the advantage that it avoids these
problems when p ≤ 3. As far as we know, this approach was first adopted in [BT]
but presumably it was known before then.
    We explain how some of the theorems on the canonical subgroup in chapter 3
of [K] can be applied to (integral models of) elliptic curves corresponding to
points in X1 (N )≥r when r = p−p/(p+1) . In fact the extra conditions imposed by
Katz on the level structure when p ≤ 3 can be removed, in the cases that we
are interested in. We firstly recall some of the results on the canonical subgroup
proved in [K], results which Katz attributes to Lubin.
    Let L be a finite extension of K and let E/L be an elliptic curve, equipped
with an embedding i : µN → E defined over L. Then the pair (E, i) defines an
L-valued point α of X1 (N )K . We define a rational number v(E, i) associated
(non-canonically) to the pair (E, i) (the definition is non-canonical because it
depends on our choices of parameters Tx ). Note first that N ≥ 5 and hence
E cannot have additive reduction. If E has multiplicative or good ordinary
reduction then define v(E, i) = 0. If however E has supersingular reduction,
then let x ∈ X1 (N )(Fp ) be the point corresponding to the reduction of (E, P ).
Recall that we have fixed a parameter Tx on the disc Bx of points above x.
Define |(E, i)| = |Tx (α)| and define v(E, i) by |(E, i)| = p−v(E,i) . Note that if
|(E, i)| is zero then technically v(E, i) = +∞ is not defined, but our arguments
will only concern elliptic curves corresponding to points near the edge of the
supersingular discs and in particular we shall never be concerned with the exact
value v(E, i) if it is at least 1. We remark here that if v(E, i) < p/(p + 1)
then one can use Theorem 3.10.7 of [K] to verify that v(E, i) is determined by
the formal group of E and is hence independent of i and of T . Note that the
calculation at this point of [K] does not depend on the fact that p ≥ 5. In
fact, although Katz has to be slightly careful when p ≤ 3 in his definition of
the canonical subgroup for an elliptic curve over a p-adic ring, Theorem 3.10.7
of [K] can be used to deduce that if we restrict ourselves to elliptic curves defined
over the integers of a finite extension of K0 , everything is independent of the
level structure that we chose. What seems to be happening is that Katz is
actually defining a rigid-analytic function on the supersingular discs, such that
v(E, i) is the valuation of the value of this function at (E, i). When p is less
than 5, the actual value of the function could depend on the choice of i, but the
valuation of this value does not, at least near the boundary of the disc. See also
the forthcoming definition of v 0 , which is in general perhaps a more canonical
measure of the “supersingularity” of E, but which depends on the choice of an
auxiliary subgroup of order p.

Remark 3.2. Geometrically speaking, the supersingular but not too supersingu-
lar elliptic curves are the ones near the outer edges of the supersingular discs Bx .
   If E is not too supersingular, then v(E, i) is independent of the finite field
extension L0 and the choice of i, and we call it v(E). In [K], Katz explains

before he can define a canonical subgroup. But because we are only working
with elliptic curves over finite extensions of K0 , Theorem 3.10.7 of [K] shows us
that the canonical subgroup will be independent of this choice. We remark also
that Katz attributes this work to Lubin.

language.
Theorem 3.3 (Katz, Lubin). For a not too supersingular elliptic curve E/L,
let H = H(E) ⊂ E be its canonical subgroup. Then H has order p. Moreover,
  (i) If v(E) = 0 then H is the finite étale subgroup of E corresponding to the
      kernel of the reduction map from E[p](L) to the p-torsion in the Néron
      model of E over OL .
 (ii) If v(E) < 1/(p + 1) then E/H is not too supersingular, and v(E/H) =
      pv(E).
(iii) If v(E) = 1/(p + 1) then E/H is too supersingular.
 (iv) If v(E) ∈ (1/(p + 1), p/(p + 1)), then E/H is again not too supersingular,
      v(E/H) = 1 − v(E), and furthermore the canonical subgroup of E/H is
      E[p]/H.
 (v) If v(E) < p/(p + 1) and C 6= H is a subgroup of E of order p, then
     v(E/C) = v(E)/p and the canonical subgroup H(E/C) of E/C is E[p]/C.
 (vi) If v(E) ≥ p/(p + 1) and C ⊂ E is a subgroup of order p, then v(E/C) =
      1/(p + 1) and the canonical subgroup H(E/C) of E/C is E[p]/C.
Proof. All these results other than (iii) are contained in either the beginning
of §3.4 of [K], or Theorem 3.10.7 of [K]. We deduce (iii) as follows. If v(E) =
1/(p + 1) then by the proof of [K] 3.10.7(2) we see that there are points on
(E/H)[p] with ordinal 1/(p2 − 1). But [K] 3.10.7(1) shows that this cannot
happen if E/H is not too supersingular.

Geometric remarks. If E has an Hn then this means geometrically that either
E has good ordinary or multiplicative reduction, or that E is supersingular
but is very close to the boundary of the supersingular disc, in some sense. The
proposition above shows that if one quotients out a not too supersingular elliptic
curve by a “non-canonical” subgroup of order pm , that is, one which has trivial
intersection with the canonical subgroup, then it becomes even less supersingu-
lar. Conversely, by Theorem 3.3, if one quotients out a not too supersingular
elliptic curve by its canonical subgroup, one usually ends up with an elliptic
curve which is “more supersingular”, and if one continues to do this then it
could happen that after a while the curve becomes too supersingular and hence
does not have a canonical subgroup. The geometry of this situation will be
clarified in the next section.

Let X1 (N ; p)an denote the rigid space associated to X1 (N ; p)K . Let ∞ denote
the cusp of X1 (N ; p) corresponding to the Tate curve Eq := Gm /q Z equipped
with the natural embedding of µN into Gm and the subgroup of pth roots of
unity in Gm . The reduction mod p of this cusp is a smooth point on one
of the components of X1 (N ; p)Fp ; call this component X∞ . The non-cuspidal
Fp -valued points on X∞ correspond to elliptic curves over Fp equipped with a
point of order N and a finite flat subgroup scheme of order p which only has
one geometric point.
    Let W0 (p) ⊂ X1 (N ; p)an denote the set of points whose reduction lies in X∞ .
Then W0 (p) is an admissible open subset of X1 (N ; p)an , and is in fact a wide-
open space, as can be seen for example by checking that the proof of Propo-
sition 3.3 of [Col1] does not use the fact that the base field is Cp . We shall
now define a function v 0 : W0 (p) → Q which is related to v but has the advan-
tage that it does not depend on the choice of uniformisers at each supersingular
point.
    Let x ∈ W0 (p) be a point. If x is a cusp then define v 0 (x) = 0. Otherwise,
x corresponds to a closed point of Y1 (N ; p)K whose reduction lies in X∞ . In
particular, there is a finite extension L/K, and an elliptic curve E/L equipped
with an embedding i of µN and a subgroup C of order p, such that the data
(E, i, C) corresponds to x. Now define v 0 (x) as follows. If E is ordinary then
define v 0 (x) = 0. If E has supersingular reduction but E is not too supersingular,
and C happens to be the canonical subgroup of E, then define v 0 (x) = v(E). If
v(E, i) ≥ p/(p + 1) and hence E has no canonical subgroup, then define v 0 (x) =
p/(p + 1). Finally, if E has supersingular reduction, and E has a canonical
subgroup but it is not C, then by part (v) of Theorem 3.3, we have v(E/C) <
1/(p + 1), and we set v 0 (x) = 1 − v(E/C, i) ∈ (p/(p + 1), 1). A case by case
check shows that v 0 does not depend on the choice of uniformising parameters
at the supersingular points (we used these choices to define v but our definitions
were independent of choice near the edges of the discs). Moreover, v 0 does not
depend on i, and hence we can speak about v 0 (E, C) when we mean v 0 (x) for
some point x corresponding to (E, i, C) for some embedding i of µN .
    A more concise definition of v 0 is the following: away from the supersingular
region, v 0 = 0. In the supersingular region, v 0 (E, C) = v(E) if C is the canonical
subgroup of E, and in general v 0 (E, C) + v 0 (E/C, E[p]/C) = 1, where we note
that either C or E[p]/C are canonical by part (vi) of Theorem 3.3.
    We explain another way of thinking about the function v 0 . On a supersin-
gular annulus, v 0 looks rather like the valuation of an isomorphism between the
annulus and the subset {1/p < |z| < 1} of affine 1-space. In fact, things are
slightly more complicated, but not overly so. Let q be a rational number such
that 1/2 < q < p/(p + 1), and let r = p−q . Consider the preimage of X1 (N )≥r
under π1 in X1 (N, p)an . This preimage is two disjoint affinoids. Moreover, the
component X1 (N, p)≥r of this preimage containing the cusp ∞ maps isomorphi-
cally down to X1 (N )≥r by π1 again, by the theory of the canonical subgroup.


                                         12
The pullbacks of the parameters Tx that we chose on the supersingular discs of
X1 (N )an give us a function on the supersingular part of X1 (N, p)≥r , and on a
point in this region, v 0 agrees with the valuation of this function.
    Things are equally simple at the other end of the annulus: if we consider
the preimage of X1 (N )≥r under π2 , then the component not containing ∞ is
an affinoid mapping one-to-one down to X1 (N )≥r , and pulling back the func-
tions Tx onto the supersingular annuli in this affinoid gives us a function defined
on these annuli, such that 1 − v 0 agrees with the valuation of this function. So
“at either end” of the supersingular annuli in X1 (N, p), the function v 0 looks
like the valuation of an isomorphism to an explicit annulus in the affine line,
and part (iv) of Theorem 3.3 guarantees that things match up in the middle of
the annuli.

Proposition 4.1. All of the Vn are admissible open affinoid subsets of the curve

In this section we explain a construction the Hecke operator Up in the rigid-

eigenvectors for Up satisfy certain analytic continuation properties.
    Let C and D be smooth proper geometrically irreducible algebraic curves

6     More analytic continuation.
We do not know how to improve on the results of Section 5 in the level N p
cases, that is, we do not know whether one can extend eigenforms beyond the
supersingular locus. But if we are working on level N pm with m > 1 then,
rather surprisingly, we can do even better, and the object of this section is to
explain how to do so.
